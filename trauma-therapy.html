Does this count?

Most people don't walk in saying the word trauma.

They say they can't sleep. They say they snap at people they love, or feel nothing at a funeral and far too much in a checkout line. They say they were fine for years and then, out of nowhere, weren't.

Some people can point to one event. Others describe a whole childhood, a marriage, or a job that slowly wore them down. Both count. You don't have to prove your story is bad enough to deserve help.

If you're wondering whether it counts, that's usually reason enough to talk.

How the work goes

Steady ground first. The harder material can wait.

The early sessions are about building enough steadiness that a hard conversation doesn't flatten you for the rest of the week. For some people that takes a few sessions. For others it takes a few months. Neither is a problem.

I won't ask you to describe the worst thing that ever happened to you. Not in the first session, and not until you decide you want to. A lot of healing happens without a detailed retelling.

We also notice what your body does while we talk: the held breath, the fog that rolls in, the sudden urge to change the subject. None of that means you're bad at therapy. It's information, and we use it.

Every 8 to 12 weeks we stop and look at what's actually different. If something isn't working, we change it.

When help has hurt

If the people who were supposed to help made it worse.

Many of the people who find me have been through a system first, whether medical, legal, school or a treatment program. Some were required to be here. Some were called difficult for reacting the way anyone would. Being wary of therapy after that makes sense to me.

I'd rather you come in skeptical than not come in at all. I wrote more about that in When Help Has Hurt Before, and about therapy that wasn't your idea in When Someone Else Decided You Should Be Here.
