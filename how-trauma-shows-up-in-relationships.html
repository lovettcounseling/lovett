How Trauma Shows Up in Relationships

Most couples don't come in talking about trauma. They come in talking about the dishes, or the tone of a text, or the way one of them goes quiet for two days after a fight that seemed small. In a relationship, trauma rarely announces itself. It shows up in reactions that seem too big, or too small, for what just happened.

Reactions that don't match the moment

A voice gets a little louder, and the other partner is suddenly somewhere else: flat, far away, agreeing to anything. A plan changes at the last minute and it lands like a betrayal. A hand on the shoulder from behind gets a flinch neither of you saw coming.

From the inside, these reactions make sense. They were learned somewhere it paid to react fast. From the outside, especially to a partner who had nothing to do with the original harm, they can look like overreacting, or like not caring at all.

Neither reading is quite right.

What I see most often in the room

Shutting down instead of fighting. If conflict was once dangerous, the body ends it the fastest way it knows: going numb, going silent, leaving the room. The other partner experiences this as being abandoned halfway through a sentence.

Scanning for signs. A changed tone, a late reply, a door closed a little too hard. Someone who grew up predicting other people's moods keeps doing it as an adult, and it wears out both people.

Needing a say in small things. The thermostat, the weekend plan, which seat at the restaurant. It can look rigid. Often it's about having some control after a long stretch of having none.

Pulling away when things are good. Closeness can feel riskier than distance. So a great Saturday gets followed by a strange, cold Monday, and nobody can say why.

None of this makes someone a bad partner. It makes them someone who adapted.

If you're the one with the history

You may already know some of this about yourself, and know that knowing it doesn't stop it. That's normal. Understanding usually comes first, and the change comes later, with practice.

What helps is catching it earlier: the tight chest, the urge to leave, the fog rolling in. If you can name it in one sentence, something like "I'm getting flooded and I need ten minutes," your partner gets something other than silence to respond to.

Then come back. The return matters more than the break.

If you're the partner on the other side of it

It's hard to love someone and keep getting cast in a role you never auditioned for. You're allowed to find that painful. You're also not responsible for repairing something that happened before you met.

Two things tend to help. Stop debating whether the reaction is reasonable, because that conversation goes nowhere. And ask what would help next time when things are calm, not in the middle of it.

Being patient doesn't mean having no needs of your own.

Where couples therapy comes in

In couples therapy, I'm not deciding who's right. I slow down the moments that usually escalate so both of you can see what's happening underneath the argument. It's trauma-informed, which means we move at a pace both of you can handle, and nobody is pushed to share their history before they're ready.

Sometimes one person starts with individual therapy and the other joins later. That's common, and it works. If the trauma side feels like the bigger piece, there's more on how I approach it on the trauma therapy page.

All sessions are by secure video anywhere in Colorado, so you can join from the same couch or from two different places.

A place to start

You don't need a name for what's happening to reach out. The free fifteen-minute call is a good place to describe the loop you keep landing in and ask whether this kind of work fits. Couples sessions are $200 for 60 minutes; the fees page has the rest.
