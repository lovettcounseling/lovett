Burnout or Just a Hard Season? How to Tell the Difference

"I'm just really stressed right now" is one of the most common things I hear in a first session — and about half the time, what's actually underneath it isn't stress. It's burnout. They feel similar from the inside, but they're different conditions, and they don't respond to the same fixes.

The Difference Between Stress and Burnout

Stress, broadly, is too much: too many demands, too little time, a nervous system running hot. It's uncomfortable, but it's usually tied to something specific, and it tends to ease once the pressure lifts — the deadline passes, the busy season ends, the crisis resolves.

Burnout is different. It's not too much, it's too empty. It's what happens after stress has been sustained long enough that your system starts conserving resources by shutting things down — motivation, empathy, patience, even physical energy. Where stress feels like urgency, burnout feels like flatness. You can be burned out and not busy at all; the exhaustion doesn't lift with a weekend off, because it's not really about the workload anymore.

Signs Burnout Has Set In

  • Physical: exhaustion that sleep doesn't touch, getting sick more often, tension headaches or a tight jaw that's become background noise.
  • Emotional: a flatness or cynicism that feels unfamiliar, irritability with people you usually enjoy, dread that shows up before you've even started the day.
  • Cognitive: trouble concentrating, forgetting things that used to be automatic, a sense of going through the motions.
  • Relational: withdrawing from people, less patience at home, feeling resentful toward people who are asking completely reasonable things of you.

Why Helping Professionals Hit This Especially Hard

I see this constantly in the nurses, teachers, therapists, social workers, and first responders I work with, both individually and through the trauma-informed trainings I run for organizations. When your job requires you to regulate other people's nervous systems all day — a classroom, a care team, a crisis line — there's often nothing left to regulate your own by the time you get home. Burnout in these roles isn't a personal failing or a sign you picked the wrong career. It's a predictable outcome of chronic emotional labor without enough structural support to offset it.

What Actually Helps

Not, unfortunately, a bubble bath. Burnout responds to a few specific things: naming it accurately instead of pushing through under the "stress" label, which tends to lead to more of the same coping strategies that aren't working. Boundaries that are actually enforced, not just intended. Enough rest that your system can complete its stress cycle, not just distract from it. And often, therapy that looks at what's underneath the burnout — sometimes it's workload, but just as often it's old patterns around worth, responsibility, or difficulty saying no that make the workload harder to carry than it has to be.

When to Reach Out

If you're reading this and recognizing yourself, that's usually a good sign it's time to talk to someone — not because burnout means something is wrong with you, but because it's genuinely hard to see your own patterns clearly from inside them. Individual therapy can help you figure out what's yours to change and what needs a bigger structural fix. And if you're noticing this pattern across a whole team or organization, that's exactly what the consulting and training work is for.
