What Actually Happens in a First Therapy Session

People rehearse. They show up with the whole history organized, worried they'll waste the hour or say it wrong. You don't need to do that. Here is what the first session is actually like, so you can stop preparing for it.

Before you arrive

You'll get intake paperwork by email: contact details, a consent form, the practice policies, payment information. It takes about fifteen minutes and it's done before the session so the hour stays yours.

If anything in it confuses you, leave it blank and we'll go through it together.

The first ten minutes

Housekeeping. I'll go over confidentiality and its limits, how payment works, how to reach me between sessions, and what happens if you need to cancel. It's not thrilling, and it matters. You should know the rules of the room you're sitting in.

Then I'll ask some version of: what made you reach out now?

Why that question, and not "tell me about your childhood"

Something shifted recently. Maybe a fight, a diagnosis, a birthday, a week where you couldn't get out of bed, or a slow accumulation that finally got loud enough. That shift tells me more than a chronological history would.

We'll get to the history. It usually comes out on its own, in pieces, over weeks.

What I won't do

I won't ask you to describe the worst thing that ever happened to you. Not in the first session, and not until you decide you want to.

I won't hand you a diagnosis on the way out the door. I won't tell you what you should do about your marriage or your job after fifty minutes.

And I won't be taking notes the entire time with my eyes on a screen.

If you cry, or if you don't

Both are completely normal and neither means anything about whether therapy is working.

Some people cry before they've finished the first sentence. Some people tell the hardest story of their life in a flat, efficient voice and feel strange about it afterward. That flatness is usually protective, and it's information, not a problem.

How to tell if it's a fit

Pay attention to how you feel in the twenty minutes after, not during. During is usually just adrenaline.

Afterward, ask yourself: did I feel listened to, or processed? Did I say one true thing I hadn't planned to say? Would I be willing to come back and be a little more honest than I was today?

Research on therapy outcomes keeps landing on the same finding: the relationship between the two people in the room predicts more than the specific method does. Fit isn't a nice extra. It's the active ingredient.

You're allowed to not come back

If it isn't right, you can say so, or you can simply not schedule again. You don't owe me an explanation, and I'm glad to give you other names.

Starting with a free fifteen-minute call takes most of the pressure off. You get a sense of how I talk and think before anyone books anything. That's what it's there for.
